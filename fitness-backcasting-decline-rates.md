# Age-Related Decline Rates by Training Status: Research Summary for Backcasting

## 1. VO2 Max Decline in Masters Athletes

### The core finding: training status matters more than age

The commonly cited "5% per decade" for trained athletes is roughly correct, but the real picture is more nuanced. The key insight from the literature is that **training volume explains 54% of the variance in VO2max decline in men and 39% in women** -- meaning the decline rate is not fixed, it is a function of how much you keep training.

### Specific decline rates by training status (VO2max)

| Training Status | Decline Per Decade | Key Source |
|---|---|---|
| Highly trained, maintained volume | **5-6.5%** | Tanaka & Seals 2008; longitudinal masters data |
| Moderately trained, reduced volume | **7-10%** (~26% in some longitudinal cohorts who dropped substantially) | Pollock 1997; MDPI 2022 review |
| Sedentary / ceased training | **10-15%** (up to 46% per decade in those who became fully inactive) | Rogers et al. 1990; Pollock 1997 |
| General population average (blended) | **~10% per decade** (~1% per year) | FRIEND Registry; general epidemiological data |

### The Pollock 20-year longitudinal data (1997)

The landmark study followed 21 masters track athletes over 20 years (mean ages 50 -> 60 -> 70):

- **High group** (remained elite, n=9): 8% decline first decade, 15% decline second decade
- **Moderate group** (continued moderate-to-rigorous training, n=10): 13% decline first decade, 14% decline second decade
- **Low group** (greatly reduced training, n=2): 18% decline first decade, 34% decline second decade

Key takeaway: Even in the "high" group that maintained elite training, the decline **accelerated** from 8% to 15% in the second decade (ages 60-70). This is critical for backcasting.

### Sport-specific differences

- **Swimming**: ~0.6% per year (~6% per decade) up to age 70-75, then accelerates in quadratic fashion
- **Running**: Curvilinear decline from age 35, modest until 50-60, then steeper
- **Cycling**: Similar pattern to running, with VO2max being the primary limiter
- **Cross-sectional masters world records**: ~7.2% per decade overall, but men 6.6%/decade vs women 7.7%/decade

### FRIEND Registry reference values (general population, 50th percentile)

| Age Group | Men (ml/kg/min) | Women (ml/kg/min) |
|---|---|---|
| 20-29 | 48.0 | 37.6 |
| 70-79 | 24.4 | 18.3 |

This represents a ~49% total decline in men and ~51% total decline in women over 50 years (~10% per decade, confirming the population average).

## 2. Strength Decline in Masters Athletes / Lifelong Exercisers

### The Wroblewski study (PMID 22030953) -- confirmed and extended

The Wroblewski 2011 study of 40 masters athletes (ages 40-81, training 4-5x/week) found:

- **Mid-thigh muscle area did NOT decline significantly with age** (p=0.31)
- **Quadriceps peak torque did NOT decline significantly with age**
- **Specific strength (strength per unit muscle area) did NOT decline significantly** (p=0.06)
- Total body fat percentage DID increase with age (p=0.003)

The study's conclusion: "These declines may signal the effect of chronic disuse rather than muscle aging."

### Powerlifting-specific data (Anton et al. 2004; longitudinal growth modeling 2023)

- **Male masters powerlifters over 69**: ~0.35% strength loss per year (~3.5% per decade)
- **Female masters powerlifters over 59**: Still showing 2.5-5.0% strength IMPROVEMENT
- Powerlifting performance shows **linear** decline after age 40 (contrast with Olympic weightlifting which is curvilinear/steeper)
- Relative strength losses from Open to Masters 1 (age 40-49): ~11% squat, ~8% bench, ~10% deadlift, ~11% total

### Olympic weightlifting vs powerlifting

- Weightlifting declines are **curvilinear** and steeper than powerlifting
- Powerlifting declines are **linear** and more gradual
- Male masters weightlifters: ~1.0-1.5% per year deterioration (Meltzer)
- The difference is attributed to the greater power/speed demands of Olympic lifts vs pure strength

### General sarcopenia rates (untrained population)

- After age 30: 3-5% muscle mass loss per decade
- After age 60: 3-8% per decade, accelerating
- ~1% per year linear decline from age 30 (general population)
- **Inactive people lose the most**; resistance training can reverse losses even in the elderly

### Neuromuscular power vs muscle mass

- Masters athletes lose **neuromuscular power at ~8% per decade**
- Muscle mass declines at ~5% per decade in trained populations
- The gap (power declining faster than mass) reflects neural/speed-of-contraction losses

## 3. Is the Decline Linear? The "Cliff Age" Question

### The answer: NO, it is not linear. There IS an acceleration point.

The data consistently shows a **two-phase or curvilinear pattern**:

**Phase 1 (ages ~35 to 60-70):**
- Modest, roughly linear decline
- VO2max: ~5-6% per decade if maintaining training
- Strength: minimal to none in trained populations
- Masters world records show ~6-7% per decade

**Phase 2 (after ~70-75):**
- Accelerated, exponential/quadratic decline
- VO2max: can jump to 15-25% per decade even in trained athletes
- Swimming performance shifts from 0.6%/year to quadratic acceleration
- Pollock's "high" group went from 8% to 15% per decade
- Sprint, middle/long-distance running records show acceleration after 70
- CRF decline increases to 15-20%/decade for women and 20-25%/decade for men over 70

**Important nuance**: Throwing and jumping disciplines maintain a more linear decline even past 70, suggesting the acceleration is more pronounced in activities with high cardiorespiratory demands.

### The "cliff" is around age 70-75

This is consistent across:
- Masters swimming world records (quadratic acceleration after 70-75)
- Pollock longitudinal data (acceleration in 60-70 decade)
- Cross-sectional masters running records
- General CRF decline data

### Physiological mechanisms for the acceleration

- Until late middle age: primarily reduced cardiac output (lower HRmax)
- After ~70: skeletal muscle oxidative capacity decline compounds the cardiac limitation
- Mitochondrial dysfunction becomes increasingly important
- Motor unit loss and denervation accelerate
- HRmax decline: ~5-7 beats/min/decade (linear, independent of training)

## 4. The Anabolic Resistance Problem

### Baseline protein synthesis: NOT different with age

Surprisingly, basal rates of muscle protein synthesis and breakdown are **unchanged** with healthy aging. The problem is not resting metabolism -- it is the **response to stimuli**.

### The blunted response -- specific numbers

| Metric | Young (~24 yrs) | Older (~70 yrs) | Magnitude of Deficit |
|---|---|---|---|
| MPS response to 20g protein | +75% above baseline | +21% above baseline | ~3.6x less responsive |
| Overall MPS response to feeding | -- | -- | >3-fold smaller capability to elevate MPS |
| MPS response to resistance exercise (AUC) | Reference | 30% lower | ~30% blunted (Kumar 2009) |
| MPS timing after exercise | Peaks at 1-3h | Peaks at 3-6h | Delayed response |
| Protein dose for equivalent MPS | 20g whey | 40g whey | 2x dose required |

### Kumar 2009 (PMID 19001042) -- the dose-response study

- Studied young (24 +/- 6 yrs) and old (70 +/- 5 yrs) men
- Dose-response to resistance exercise at 20-90% 1RM was sigmoidal in both groups
- **Older men's total MPS response was 30% lower** (area under the curve)
- p70s6K and 4EBP1 phosphorylation at 60-90% 1RM was blunted in older men
- Both groups plateaued at 60% 1RM intensity -- but older men at a lower plateau

### Can exercise overcome anabolic resistance?

**Yes, partially:**
- Physical activity before protein intake increases amino acid utilization in older muscle
- Higher protein doses (40g vs 20g) can achieve MPS rates similar to young adults
- Habitual physical activity may be "fundamental to maintain anabolic responsiveness"
- Life-long exercisers show better preserved anabolic signaling than sedentary older adults

### Practical implication for backcasting

For someone who trains consistently, anabolic resistance is a **real but manageable** factor. The 30% MPS deficit can be partially compensated with higher protein doses and maintained training intensity. For sedentary individuals, the deficit is larger and compounds with disuse atrophy.

## 5. Sex-Specific Decline Rates in Trained Populations

### VO2max decline

| Population | Men | Women |
|---|---|---|
| Masters athletes (overall performance) | 6.6% per decade | 7.7% per decade |
| Absolute VO2max decline | Greater (L/min) | Smaller (L/min) |
| Relative decline (% per decade) | Similar or slightly less | Similar or slightly more |
| After age 70 (CRF decline) | 20-25% per decade | 15-20% per decade |

**Key finding**: Men decline faster in absolute terms but women show slightly greater percentage decline in masters competition performance. After age 70, men's CRF decline actually exceeds women's.

### Strength decline

- Male masters powerlifters over 69: ~0.35% per year loss
- Female masters powerlifters over 59: still gaining 2.5-5.0%
- **Women may actually have an advantage** in maintaining strength with training into their 60s

### Muscle mass decline

- Males: 0.8-1.3% muscle loss per year (general population)
- Females: 0.5-0.8% muscle loss per year (general population)
- Men lose muscle mass faster in absolute terms

### Swimming performance decline by sex

- Men decline more slowly than women overall
- Sex difference is largest in older age groups (85-89) and shorter events
- At younger masters ages, women's longitudinal decline is actually less than world record data would predict

### Overall pattern

The sex difference in trained populations is **smaller than in the general population**. Training narrows the gap. For backcasting purposes, applying a small modifier (~1% additional decline per decade for women vs men in VO2max) is reasonable, but the difference is modest in trained populations.

## 6. Recommended Backcasting Formulas

### The problem with simple compound rates

A single compound rate (like Attia's 15%/decade) fails because:
1. It blends sedentary and active populations
2. It assumes linear decline when decline is actually curvilinear
3. It does not account for training status
4. It does not capture the acceleration after age 70

### Recommended approach: Piecewise compound decline model

Use different rates for different age phases and training statuses.

### VO2max Backcasting Rates

**Phase 1: Age 30-70 (pre-acceleration)**

| Training Status | Annual Decline | Per Decade |
|---|---|---|
| A) Consistently trained (strength + cardio) | 0.5%/year | ~5% |
| B) Moderately active | 0.8%/year | ~8% |
| C) Sedentary | 1.2%/year | ~12% |

**Phase 2: Age 70+ (post-acceleration)**

| Training Status | Annual Decline | Per Decade |
|---|---|---|
| A) Consistently trained | 1.2%/year | ~12% |
| B) Moderately active | 1.8%/year | ~17% |
| C) Sedentary | 2.5%/year | ~23% |

### Strength / Lean Mass Backcasting Rates

**Phase 1: Age 30-70**

| Training Status | Annual Muscle Mass Decline | Annual Strength Decline |
|---|---|---|
| A) Consistently trained (incl. resistance) | ~0.1%/year (near zero) | ~0.3%/year |
| B) Moderately active | 0.5%/year | 0.8%/year |
| C) Sedentary | 1.0%/year | 1.5%/year |

**Phase 2: Age 70+**

| Training Status | Annual Muscle Mass Decline | Annual Strength Decline |
|---|---|---|
| A) Consistently trained | 0.5%/year | 0.8%/year |
| B) Moderately active | 1.0%/year | 1.5%/year |
| C) Sedentary | 1.5-2.0%/year | 2.0-2.5%/year |

### Power / Explosiveness Backcasting Rates

Power declines faster than strength due to neural and speed-of-contraction factors:

| Training Status | Per Decade (all ages) |
|---|---|
| A) Consistently trained | ~8% |
| B) Moderately active | ~12% |
| C) Sedentary | ~15-18% |

### Backcasting Formula (compound decline)

For any metric at a target age, given a current value:

```
value_at_age = current_value * (1 - annual_rate) ^ (age_difference)
```

For the piecewise model:

```
If current_age < 70 and target_age < 70:
    value = current * (1 - phase1_rate) ^ years

If current_age < 70 and target_age >= 70:
    years_in_phase1 = 70 - current_age
    years_in_phase2 = target_age - 70
    value = current * (1 - phase1_rate)^years_in_phase1 * (1 - phase2_rate)^years_in_phase2

If current_age >= 70:
    value = current * (1 - phase2_rate) ^ years
```

### Example: Backcasting a 45-year-old consistently trained person to age 80

VO2max:
- Phase 1 (45 to 70, 25 years): (1 - 0.005)^25 = 0.882 (11.8% total decline)
- Phase 2 (70 to 80, 10 years): (1 - 0.012)^10 = 0.886 (11.4% total decline)
- Total: 0.882 * 0.886 = 0.781 = **21.9% total decline over 35 years**

Compare to Attia's 15%/decade applied uniformly: (1 - 0.015)^35 = 0.589 = **41.1% decline** -- almost double. This illustrates why population averages dramatically overestimate decline for trained individuals.
